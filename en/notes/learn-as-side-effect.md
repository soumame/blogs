soumame

> I guess people get interested in the world from things like that. I imagine people who know a lot about how the Internet works at university probably got interested from places like that.


## Where do my insights originate?

I think discoveries can come not only from hobbies or intentional exploration, but also unexpectedly from other places.

soumame
> I once had a [[en/works/yago.inc|part-time job teaching Minecraft at a programming school]], and I ran into problems there. From that I went through things like [[en/accomplishments/mitoujr-2024|Mitou Junior]] and came to enjoy software development.
> The original purpose was just to do the assigned tasks as a part-timer and get paid, but the inconveniences I noticed and small observations drifted away from the main job, and I think those connected to what I like and want to do more of.


## What impact has the arrival of AI had?

Honestly, recently I feel like those kinds of insights have decreased a bit.

soumame
> For example, there's an app I'm building at [[en/works/keio|university]], and I've had AI implement more than half of it. But sometimes my understanding of the deeper parts I should know is lacking, and when that's pointed out I end up saying, "AI made it so I don't know."

Seriously, I think this is a really serious problem. But we can't stop.

Companies and researchers use it as a matter of course, and at least in software development the necessary cost and personal time are overwhelmingly reduced. When everyone around you is using AI, you can't keep writing code by hand without using AI.

However, the joy of writing code and the sudden insights you get while thinking might have disappeared along with the coding experience.

soumame
> When I did [[en/accomplishments/mitoujr-2024|Mitou Junior]] in 2024, ChatGPT was still kind of dumb and couldn't autonomously write code — good old days...
> People were saying things like "GPT-4 is way too smart, this will change the world," or "Google Bard is being renamed to Gemini, how long will it last?" or "Anthropic released Claude 3 Haiku"... it was a noisy time.

Back then there weren't many coding agents and they weren't very useful — nostalgic. I used to copy and paste code from AI and had to think about why it worked. Because AI was unreliable, I took a lot of detours.

But when did it happen? Before I knew it, I feel like I've been leaving everything to AI.

soumame
> If I recall, around the summer of 2024 people like Cline and Devin appeared and the engineering community got excited. Editors integrated with AI like Cursor started to rise...

soumame
> Right, then from autumn to winter something called GPT o1 came out and LLMs started to do "reasoning."

soumame
> I feel like you could make a timeline out of just the last few years. When COVID-19 spread the world changed a lot and it felt hard to keep up, but this is changing at least as fast if not faster. And humans are adapting more than expected.

soumame
> Then o3 came out, the DeepSeek shock hit, Claude Opus 4 appeared and everyone was surprised, and then GPT5, 5.5... they kept evolving.

They've evolved to be able to do everything and know more than me, so I might be leaving everything to them.


## I'm scared

Given how much has changed, I even wonder if they will surpass humans this year. It's terrifying. Can I keep up?

But humans are simpler than I thought, so we probably won't die from worrying too much. We're just using whatever convenient tools are available.


soumame
> Actually, they've already surpassed humans in some sense. It's questionable whether they've surpassed every person on Earth, but I've never seen a human with such broad knowledge who can answer like a top expert in any field. They solve things I couldn't figure out even after thinking for a long time, in an instant.


## What is the meaning of learning...?

It feels uncertain whether studying technology will still be useful in society. There are areas AI isn't great at right now (hardware development, low-level stuff, OS-related work), but I think it's only a matter of time before those aren't safe havens either.

### I don't even know what I want to research

I once tried using LLMs for programming education and experimented a lot, thinking I might continue that in university. I even wrote that on my entrance documents. But after a few months at university, AI can make the same things I've been building.

Making things is fun, of course. But I feel the enjoyment of the process has decreased significantly, and because I have AI do it, the byproducts I used to get are gone.

So how should I acquire skills? If you let AI do everything, in the worst case you won't be able to solve problems yourself and end up depending entirely on AI. Society could still run that way, I guess.

soumame
> Maybe even if everyone is softened by AI, if everyone uses it, society will still keep going.

But is it okay to become dependent on AI?

soumame
> We can't do anything without computers now, and if they suddenly disappeared humans probably couldn't do anything. I wonder if this is the same level of dependency.

I don't think dependence itself is bad if it makes something better.
What gets better? Human technical ability? Human happiness?
I once wrote about [[en/notes/why-human-move|Why People Move]], but at this rate you might lose sight of why you're alive. What are we living for?

## Should I take the tedious path?

In the end, I've been thinking that to keep enjoying creating things I should probably take the tedious path. Try deliberately not using AI, or ask AI tons of questions. That way you can enjoy the process, and the sense of achievement when you finish is incredible.

### Because of capitalism

soumame
> I admit I don't understand money well, so I don't know if this is right.

In the end, I think capitalism is partly to blame for this dependence. If you sell what you make you get money, and you can buy things with money. So if you use AI to make things in a shorter time and at lower cost and sell them, you get more money and accelerate growth.

The same thing happens in schools. When homework is assigned, everyone puts it into AI as if it's obvious. The teacher says don't use AI, so students run it through paraphrasing tools to hide that it's from AI and pretend it's their own answer. But they're learning nothing. Still, everyone keeps doing it because grades determine your path in life.

soumame
> Maybe people just think you'll get better scores if you use AI.

Even if you graduate having learned nothing but got high scores, at least in today's Japan that's accepted. Companies often don't use university knowledge on the job, and they don't hire for knowledge per se. In fact, because new hires haven't had extra information input, companies can hire them as fresh graduates and tune them to their needs.

If there's something this convenient, people will gather money into it, concentrating resources in one place — that's capitalism.

### Falling behind by taking detours

Honestly I want to enjoy the tedious stuff, but I feel like I can't take many detours. To be blunt, I have AI do university assignments too.

soumame
> Even though I know I should spend time on them, there are things I want to do... wait, does this mean going to university even make sense?

Fortunately, other things I want to do are research-group-related, so they're still within the university. [[en/works/keio|My university]] allows cross-disciplinary activities and lets first-years do what they like (if they have something), so it might be relatively easy to find what you want to do later within the university.

That said, by skipping tedious or unwanted courses with AI just to get credits, I might be missing things I could have discovered there.

When you're forced to compete in a short life, should you take detours? But being human is what allows detours.

soumame
> That said, it's just that current LLMs can't do it yet — eventually there will probably be systems that think about unrelated things and make new discoveries...

But in a world that doesn't value aimless detours and demands concrete results, it's hard. AI is already like an expert in certain fields, so as a collective human intelligence we might need to wander aimlessly to make new discoveries. But without goals it's difficult.


## So what am I trying to say?

I probably want to take more detours and I enjoy them. But the world is very competitive (as it always has been), and recently AI has started doing what humans do, so I think it's become harder to find new things by meandering through tedious tasks. Ideally I'd like to live taking those detours, but the chance of producing concrete results is low. Maybe once AI starts producing results in place of humans, I should focus on the disgustingly tedious processes AI doesn't want to do, meander around, and think. Maybe we should steer society to change that. Also, changing the system so people are rewarded when they produce results might let people live happily. (There would be considerable side effects, so I can't say it's all good.)
