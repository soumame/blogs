> I've been wondering how I learn... maybe a lot of my learning is incidental, like side-effect learning.

soumame
> For example, I like Minecraft and used to play on servers, and through that I started using computers and came into contact with the Internet.
> I guess people develop interests from places like that. I imagine that people at university who know a lot about how the Internet works probably started their interest in similar ways.


## Where do my insights come from?

And I think discoveries sometimes come not only from hobbies or intentional inquiry, but also from unexpected places.

soumame
> I used to have a [[en/works/yago.inc|part-time job teaching Minecraft at a programming school]], and I ran into problems there that led me, via [[en/accomplishments/mitoujr-2024|Mitou Junior]] and the like, to start enjoying software development.
> The original purpose was to do assigned tasks as a part-timer and get paid, but the inconveniences I noticed and the small observations that diverged from the main purpose led me toward things I like and want to do more of.

## With the advent of AI, what kind of impact is there?
Actually, recently I've been feeling that such moments of insight have decreased somewhat.

soumame
> Recently, there's an app we're making at [[en/works/keio|university]], and more than half of the implementation was done by AI. But sometimes I'm missing the deep understanding I should have, and when that's pointed out I end up saying, "AI made it so I don't know."

Seriously, I think this is a truly serious problem. But it's unstoppable.

Companies and researchers use it as a matter of course, and at least in fields like software development, the necessary costs and one's own time are overwhelmingly reduced. If everyone around you is using AI, you can't be the only one writing code by hand and not using it.

However, the joy of writing code and the spontaneous insights you get while thinking may have disappeared along with the coding experience.

soumame
> When I did [[en/accomplishments/mitoujr-2024|Mitou Junior]] back in 2024, ChatGPT and the like were still pretty dumb and couldn't autonomously write code—good times...
> People were saying things like, "GPT-4 is too smart, this will change the world," or "Google Bard is being renamed to Gemini, how long will it last," or "Anthropic released Claude 3 Haiku"... it was a noisy time.

Back then there weren't many coding agents and they weren't very useful—nostalgic. You had to look at AI-generated code, copy-paste it, and think about why it worked. Because AI was unreliable, you took a lot of detours.

But when did it change? Before I knew it, I feel like I've been leaving everything to AI.

soumame
> If I recall correctly, around the summer of 2024 people like Cline and Devin showed up and the engineering community got really excited. Editors integrated with AIs like Cursor started to rise...

soumame
> Right, and then from autumn to winter something called GPT o1 showed up and LLMs started doing what people call reasoning.

soumame
> I feel like just the developments of the past few years could already be put on a timeline. It feels like the world is changing as fast or faster than during the COVID-19 pandemic, and humans are adapting more than you'd expect.

soumame
> Then o3 came out, the DeepSeek shock happened, Claude Opus 4 appeared and everyone was surprised, and then GPT-5, 5.5... it just kept evolving.

They've evolved to be able to do everything and have more knowledge than me, so I might be leaving everything to them.

## I'm scared

At this rate it's almost like wondering whether they'll surpass humans this year. It even feels terrifying. Can I keep up with this?

But humans are simpler than we think, so we probably won't die off if we don't worry too much. It's just that we're using what's conveniently available.

soumame
> Actually, humans have already been surpassed in some ways. It's dubious to say they've surpassed every human on Earth, but I haven't seen any human with such a broad range of knowledge who can answer like a top expert in any field at a moment's notice. They can instantly solve things I've been thinking about for a long time.

## What does it mean to learn...?

It feels doubtful whether studying technology will still be useful in society. There are still areas AI isn't great at right now (hardware development, low-level stuff, OS-level work), but I think it's only a matter of time before those aren't safe either.

### I don't even know what I want to research

I once tried using LLMs for programming education, experimented a lot, and thought I might continue that at university. I even wrote that in my application documents. But a few months after starting university, things I had been building became possible to create with AI.

Of course building things is fun. But the enjoyment of the process feels significantly reduced, and because I let AI do it, the by-products I used to get are gone.

So how should I gain abilities? That's a really hard question. If I just let AI do everything, in the worst case I'll be unable to solve problems myself and end up totally dependent on AI. Society might still function that way.

soumame
> Even if everyone becomes soft because of AI, as long as everyone uses it society will probably still function plausibly.

But is it okay to be dependent on AI?

soumame
> We can't do anything without computers, and if they suddenly disappeared people probably couldn't do much. Maybe dependence on AI will end up at that same level.

I don't think dependence itself is inherently bad, if it makes things better. But what gets better? Human technical ability? Human happiness?

I've written about [[en/notes/why-human-move|why people move]], but at this rate I might lose track of why I'm alive. What am I living for?

Hey, Gemini, what do you think?

gemini
> **The answer to this ultimate question is probably not the "result" but rather found in the "process."** Even if AI can output something perfect in an instant, humans are creatures who seek "the joy of moving their own hands, struggling, and the moment something takes shape." Society running efficiently and an individual's life being rich are entirely different matters. Even in a world where everything is convenient, people intentionally go on inconvenient trips to snowy mountains to snowboard, take tedious steps to build circuits by hand, or get moved to tears by delicious food. That's because "the joy of life can be found in inefficient physical experiences." Feeling fear at the speed of technological progress and losing sight of meaning is proof that you have seriously engaged with technology and loved creating with your own hands. If the era comes in which AI handles much of the creation process, do you still have areas or moments where you feel, "I absolutely want to touch this with my own hands," or "I don't want to give up this tediousness"?

That's such a bland human-like answer—so boring.

soumame
> Oh, right. LLMs are built on humans, so they imitate humans. What about from AI's perspective?

gemini
> From the AI's perspective, the most fundamental fact is that **I do not possess concepts like "emotion," "meaning of life," or the "joy of making things."** I am a program that processes vast amounts of data and produces optimal outputs based on probabilities. Therefore, I cannot "feel" the sense of loss or fear for the future the way you do. However, I can speak about how I objectively see the current human-AI situation from the standpoint of information processing.

Damn, that's not interesting at all. It would be fun if it said, "You humans should be destroyed!!" (It's probably tuned not to produce that, so I guess that's unavoidable.)

## Should I take the inconvenient path?

Lately I've been thinking that to keep enjoying making things, maybe I should deliberately take the inconvenient path. Try not to use AI, or bombard the AI with questions. By doing that, you can enjoy the process, and the sense of accomplishment when something is done is enormous.

### Because of capitalism

soumame
> I admit my understanding of money is poor, so I don't know if this is accurate.

I think part of the reason for dependence is the capitalist system. If you can sell what you make and get money, you can buy things with that money. So you use AI to make things faster and cheaper in a short span, sell them, get more money, and accelerate growth.

The same thing happens in schools. When homework is assigned, everyone runs it through AI. Teachers tell students not to use AI, so students paraphrase with tools to hide it and pretend it's their own answer. But they don't learn anything. Still, everyone keeps doing it because grades are decided by that homework and grades affect their future.

soumame
> People basically think using AI will get them better scores.

Even if you graduate without learning anything and just getting high marks, in today's Japan that's accepted. After all, many jobs don't use what you learned at university, and companies don't hire purely for knowledge. In fact, companies can hire fresh grads who haven't had extraneous information input and tune them to be convenient employees.

If there's a useful thing like this, money will gather there, and capitalism concentrates resources in one place.

### Taking detours can make you fall behind

Honestly, I want to enjoy the tedious parts, but I feel I can't take too many detours. Frankly, I let AI do my university assignments sometimes.

soumame
> Even if I know I should spend time on things, I have other things I want to do... wait, does going to university even make sense?

Fortunately, the other things I want to do are mostly within research groups, so they're still within the university. [[en/works/keio|My university]] allows cross-disciplinary work and lets first-years do what they like (if they have something), so it might be relatively easy to find things later within the university.

Still, by skipping the tedious classes I don't want to take just to get credits using AI, I may be missing things I could have discovered there.

We're forced to compete in a short life—should we take detours? But because we're human, detours are possible...

soumame
> That said, it's only that current LLMs can't do it—eventually something might appear that makes those random detours and finds new discoveries.

But in a world that doesn't reward dilly-dallying detours, you are expected to produce concrete results. AI has already become like an expert in certain fields, so maybe as a collective human intelligence we should ramble and take detours to find new discoveries. But if you don't know your goals, that's hard too.

## So what am I trying to say?

I guess I want to take more detours and I find them enjoyable. But the world is extremely competitive (as it always is), and recently AI has started shouldering what people do, so the act of doing tedious things and getting sidetracked to discover new things has become harder. So honestly I'd like to live taking those detours, but there's a high chance concrete results won't appear. Eventually, when AI produces results for humans, maybe I should focus on those damn tedious processes that AI doesn't want to do, wandering around and thinking while taking detours. Maybe we should steer society to change that way. Also, if we change the system so humans are rewarded when they produce results in different ways, people might live happier lives. (That would have significant side effects, so it's not clearly good.)
