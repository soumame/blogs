Lately I've been really struggling with how to record my thoughts and ideas.

It started back in elementary school when I used a note-taking tool from Microsoft called OneNote. At the time I thought it was one of the most impressive tools. It let you organize a lot of information and paste links (URLs) to the outside world (the internet).

After that I started using Notion and tools like Obsidian. These apps generally use a format called Markdown, which is easy for computers to handle, and they let you write data, link notes to each other, and bring in lots of data.

These apps have gotten much better recently: they can store data locally for fast display or save to the cloud and integrate with external services.

So at first I thought that would be fine, and I used them until fairly recently.

However, lately I haven't been opening Obsidian much. I open it a lot when I'm working or doing tasks, but I began to feel it's not great as a tool for collecting my spontaneous ideas.

In university I used Obsidian a lot because I needed to record and structure data, so I naturally opened it a lot and saw what I'd written there frequently.

But when university is on a break like now, I don't open the apps much, and the process of remembering becomes very difficult.

So I thought maybe I should build a system that reminds me, and I tried various things. In the end the systems ran on smartphones or computers. That means they aren't very useful when I'm not using those devices.

That made me think maybe owning a dedicated piece of hardware would help, so I considered an iPad. The iPad comes with the Apple Pencil, so I could scribble down things I want to remember like a notebook and look at them later.

However, this device (which I used until recently) has the problem that you have to press the power button to open it and see the contents. That may seem obvious, but for me that extra step makes it so I open it when I want to take notes, but I rarely glance at it at random and have it trigger memories.

So I've been wondering for a while if there's some better hardware.

### Stationery store

Yesterday I went to a stationery store without thinking much about it. There, they sell notebooks.

You might say, "Of course — everyone uses notebooks," but because I've been using computers a lot lately, I noticed notebooks again after a long time. I thought, "Oh, there's such a device," and felt surprised.

I occasionally go through phases where I return to paper once a year or so. This time it happened again.

So I'm writing down that I'm currently in a "paper is better" phase, and leaving this as a blog so I can remember the goodness of paper when I go back to digital.

### The advantages of paper as hardware

The great thing about paper is that what you write doesn't disappear. That's a huge point that other devices lack: other devices with displays usually replace what they show with other content, and you have to perform some operation to call up the content you wanted to see, and that operation is often relatively complicated.

With paper, you can have multiple sheets bound like a notebook. Using that, you can retrieve content with a simple action like turning pages by hand or flipping through them quickly with your finger.

Amazingly, there is no latency to display.

Also, the device is extremely thin — less than 1 mm — and very inexpensive. I'm surprised that such devices exist in the world. Maybe that's a bit dramatic, but still.

Flip through paper to find what you want to remember. There is absolutely no delay.

It's obvious — it's always displayed. Maybe it's odd to be surprised, but it's because I've been using computers exclusively recently.

### However

That said, notebooks aren't a perfect device. After using paper for a day, I ran into a problem (too soon): the experience of it always being displayed, with no concept of power, and being usable anywhere is fantastic, but it does have limits.

#### Data bloat

The trouble is that when the number of pages grows too large, you can't retrieve what you need. That's because the index of what was written on the paper is in your head. Human memory can't perfectly recall everything word-for-word, so it's very hard to remember which page contains what.

There are systems in the world for quickly retrieving notes — for example, Zettelkasten — but in my case I forget to practice such systems, and as a result I can't recall things at all.

I thought, "Ah, it would be nice if I could find data faster, instantly."

#### Integrating different media

Also, when I find an interesting article on the internet, it's difficult to put it into a notebook. This happens because I use the internet and work on computers, but it requires format conversion to represent computer-processable media on paper.

That might mean printing images and pasting them in, transcribing the content by hand, or writing down queries (like Google search queries) so I can bring that media back later.

You can't put media like videos onto paper. Well, you could try a flipbook, but still.

#### Data capacity

Paper is finite and hard to expand. Computer storage is also finite, of course, but modern devices have a lot of storage and can hold much more data than paper.

Earlier I said a notebook is very thin per page, but in terms of total storage capacity a computer wins.

### ...So?

**Wait, doesn't this mean everything would be solved by putting notes on a computer after all?**

### Fusion

Thinking this through, maybe there's no perfect place for writing down memories and notes yet. When I buy a paper notebook I think computers are better, and when I'm using a computer I think paper might be better.

So I feel we need new hardware or software for capturing my thoughts and ideas. I want something that merges the experience of writing on a real-world material with the benefits of fast computer queries, large data storage, and connections to different media.

Something close to this is displays that use electronic paper. However, they are very expensive, and I feel the technical limits of e-paper significantly compromise the paper experience. Even though refresh rates have improved, they don't perfectly integrate the instant-display experience I liked about paper with precise computer queries.

By the way, I once made a business card using e-paper. Technically it's well made, but I still think it can't beat paper. You might argue you could buy an expensive e-paper device, but the cost performance is bad.

There are systems that attach readable codes to paper and import what you write into computers. But after importing, you often have to go through processes like converting it to text, so that's also challenging.

I haven't found the answer yet about whether I should create new hardware or build a system to connect existing hardware, but it's an interesting topic, so I'll keep thinking about it.
