Lately I've been really struggling with how to record my thoughts and ideas.

It started back in elementary school when I used a Microsoft note-taking tool called OneNote. At the time, it was one of the tools I thought was pretty amazing. You could organize lots of information and paste links (URLs) from the outside world (the Internet).

After that I started using Notion and tools like Obsidian. These apps basically use Markdown, a notation that's easy to work with on computers, and they let you write data, link notes to each other, and make it easy to bring in lots of data.

These apps have gotten really good recently — they can store data locally for fast display, or save to the cloud and integrate with external services.

So at first I thought that was fine and used them until fairly recently.

However, lately I haven't been opening Obsidian that much. I do open it a lot when I'm working or doing tasks, but I started to feel it's not very suitable as a tool to collect spontaneous ideas.

I used Obsidian a lot in university classes because I needed to record and structure data, so I naturally opened it often and saw what I had written there frequently.

But now, when university is on summer break, for example, I don't open the app much and the process of remembering becomes very difficult.

So I thought maybe I should build a system that helps me remember, and I tried various experiments, but in the end the system ran on a smartphone or a computer. That means it's not very useful when I'm not using those devices.

That made me think having a dedicated piece of hardware might be nice, so I considered an iPad. The iPad comes with the Apple Pencil, so you can quickly jot down things you want to remember by hand like in a notebook and look at them later.

However, this device (which I used until recently) has the problem that you have to press the power button to open it and see the contents. That may be obvious, but for me that extra step makes it hard to glance at notes and have something randomly trigger a memory.

So I've been wondering if there's some better hardware lately.

### Stationery store
And yesterday I went to a stationery store without thinking much. There, of course, notebooks were being sold.

You might think, "Everyone uses notebooks, obviously," but because I've been using computers so much recently, seeing notebooks again felt fresh. I thought, "Oh, there really is a device like this."

I occasionally go through a phase where I return to paper about once a year. This time was one of those phases.

So I'm writing down here that I'm in a paper phase now, and leaving this as a blog so I can remember the value of paper when I go back to digital.

### The virtues of paper as hardware
The wonderful thing about paper is that what you write doesn't disappear. This is a huge point that other devices lack: devices with displays usually replace the content when they show something else. To bring back the content you wanted to see, you have to perform an operation, and those operations are often relatively complex.

With paper, you can have multiple sheets bound like a notebook. With that you can call up content with the simple action of flipping pages by hand, or by quickly flicking them with your finger.

And amazingly, there is no latency to display.

Moreover, the thickness of the device is extremely thin — less than 1 mm — and it's very cheap. I'm again surprised that such a device exists in the world. That might be a little dramatic, but still.

You flip through paper and confirm what you want to remember. There is absolutely no delay.

Of course — the content stays displayed. Maybe it's odd to be surprised, but it's because I've been using computers so much lately.

### However
That said, notebooks aren't perfect devices. One day after I returned to paper, I ran into a problem (too soon). The experience of always being able to display content, having no concept of power, and being usable anywhere is fantastic, but it still has limits.

#### Data bloat
The problem is that when the number of sheets grows too large, you can't call things up anymore. The index of what is written on the paper lives in your head. And it's very difficult for human memory to remember everything word for word, so it's hard to recall which page contains what.

There are methods in the world to enable quick retrieval from a notebook, such as Zettelkasten, but I tend to forget to actually practice them, and as a result I can't retrieve anything.

#### Integrating different media
Also, when I'm browsing the Internet and find an interesting article, it's very difficult to record it in a notebook. This problem arises because I use the Internet and work on computers: you need to convert formats to represent computer-processed media on paper.

You might print and paste an image,
transcribe it into text yourself and save it,
or write queries to bring the media back, i.e., Google search queries, into your notebook.

You can't put media like videos onto paper. Unless you make a flipbook or something.

#### Data capacity
Paper is finite and hard to expand. Computer storage is finite too, of course, but modern devices generally include lots of storage and can hold much more data than paper.

Earlier I said a single sheet of paper is extremely thin, but in terms of total storable data capacity, computers beat paper.

### ...So?
**Wait, wouldn't making notes entirely on a computer solve all this?**

### Fusion
Thinking about it, there isn't really a perfect place to record notes and memories today. When I buy a paper notebook I think a computer would be better, and when I'm using a computer I think paper might be better.

That makes me feel that we need new hardware or software as a tool to capture my thoughts and ideas. I want something that combines the experience of writing on a physical material in the real world with the computer's fast queries, large data storage, and connections to different media.

Something close to this exists in the form of electronic paper displays.

However, they're very expensive, and due to technical limitations of e-paper, I feel they significantly compromise the paper experience. Even though refresh rates have improved, they don't fully integrate the paper experience I liked (instant display) with precise computer queries.

Incidentally, I once made a business card using e-paper; the technology is well made, but I still think it can't beat paper. You could buy very expensive e-paper, but it's not cost-effective.

There are also systems that attach codes to paper for scanning and importing written content into computers. But since there's still a process to convert the captured content into text data, that's also challenging.

I don't yet know whether we should build new hardware or create systems that connect existing hardware, but it's an interesting topic and I'll keep thinking about it.
