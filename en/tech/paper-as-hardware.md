Lately I've been seriously struggling with how to record my thoughts and ideas.

It started back in elementary school when I used a note-taking tool from Microsoft called OneNote. That was one of the tools I thought was really impressive at the time. You could organize a lot of information and paste links (URLs) to the outside world (the internet).

After that I started using Notion and tools like Obsidian. These apps basically use Markdown, a notation that's easy to work with on a computer, letting you write data and link notes together so it's easy to gather lots of information.

These apps have improved a lot recently in terms of experience: they can store data locally for fast display, or save to the cloud and integrate with external services.

So at first I thought that would be fine, and I used them up until very recently.

However, I haven't been opening Obsidian much lately. I open it a lot when I'm working or doing tasks, but I've come to feel it's not suitable as a tool to jot down spontaneous thoughts.

When I'm taking university classes I used Obsidian a lot because I needed to record and structure data, so I inevitably opened it and saw what's written there frequently.

But now with university on summer break, I don't open the apps much, and the process of recalling my notes becomes very difficult.

So I thought I should build a system to help me remember, and I tried various things, but in the end those systems ran on smartphones or computers. That means they aren't very useful when I'm not using those devices.

That made me wonder if having dedicated hardware would be better, and I considered an iPad. The iPad comes with an Apple Pencil, so you can quickly jot down things you want to remember by hand like a notebook and look at them later.

However, this device (which I used until recently) has the problem that you have to press the power button to open it and see the content. Maybe that's obvious, but for me that step makes it hard to glance at it and have something trigger a memory when I'm not explicitly opening it to take notes.

So I've been thinking lately that there might be a better piece of hardware.

### Stationery store

Yesterday I went to a stationery store without thinking too deeply about it. There were notebooks for sale.

You might say, "Of course, everyone uses notebooks," but since I've been using computers a lot lately, seeing notebooks again after a long time felt new to me. I thought, "Oh, this kind of device exists."

I have a habit of reverting back to paper about once a year. This time was another one of those moments.

So I'm writing down my current move toward paper here, leaving it as a blog post so I can remember the appeal of paper when I return to digital tools.

### The advantages of paper as hardware

What's great about paper is that what you write doesn't disappear. That's a huge point that other devices don't have: displays on most devices replace the shown content when you switch to something else, and you need to perform actions to recall the content you wanted to see, and those actions are often relatively complex.

With paper, however, you can get multiple pages bound together like a notebook. Using that, you can retrieve content with the simple action of flipping pages by hand, or quickly fanning through them with your finger.

Amazingly, there's effectively zero latency to display.

Also, the device thickness is extremely thin—less than 1 mm—and it's very inexpensive. I'm surprised such a device exists in the world. Maybe that's an exaggeration, but...

You flip through pages to check what you want to remember. There's no delay at all.

It's obvious—what's written remains displayed. Maybe it's odd to be surprised, but because I've been using computers so much lately, I ended up like this.

### However

A notebook is not a perfect device. One day after I started using paper, I ran into a problem (too quickly): the experience of always being able to display something, having no concept of power, and being able to use it anywhere is wonderful, but it has limits.

#### Data bloat

The problem is that when the number of paper pages grows too large, you can't retrieve what you want. The index of what's written on those pages lives in the human brain. And human memory makes it very hard to remember everything word for word, so it's difficult to remember which page contains what.

There are methods to quickly retrieve notes—Zettelkasten, for example—but in my case I tend to forget to actually follow those methods, so in the end I can't remember anything.

I found myself wishing I could find data faster, instantly.

#### Integrating different media

Also, when I find an interesting article on the internet, it's very hard to transfer that into a notebook. This problem arises because I often work on a computer and because media that can be handled on a computer need format conversion to be represented on paper.

You could print an image and paste it in,

or transcribe it by hand into text,

or write down a query—like a Google search query—to bring that media back later.

You can't put media like video on paper. You could do something like a flipbook, but that's a stretch.

#### Data capacity

Paper is finite and hard to expand. Of course computer storage is finite too, but modern devices usually have a lot of storage and can hold far more data than paper.

I said earlier that a notebook is extremely thin per page, but in terms of total storable data it loses to computers.

### ...so?

**Wait, wouldn't creating all the notes on a computer solve everything?**

### Fusion

Thinking that through makes me feel that there isn't a perfect place to write down memories today. When I buy a paper notebook I think computers are better; when I use a computer I think paper is better.

So I felt that a new piece of hardware—or software—is necessary as a tool for recording my thoughts and ideas. I want something that combines the experience of writing on real-world materials with the computer's advantages: fast queries, large storage, and connectivity to different media.

Something close to this is an electronic paper display.

However, those are very expensive, and due to technical limits of e-paper I feel they significantly diminish the paper experience. Even though refresh rates have improved, e-paper doesn't perfectly integrate the instant display experience of paper with the precise querying of computers.

Incidentally, I once made a business card using e-paper. Technically it's quite well made, but I still don't think it beats paper. Maybe buying high-end e-paper would help, but the cost-performance is poor.

There are also systems that attach codes to paper so you can digitize what you write. But after importing there's still a process to convert it into text, so that's also tricky.

I don't yet have an answer whether we should build new hardware or create systems that connect existing hardware, but it's an interesting topic, and I'll keep thinking about it.
